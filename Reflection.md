## 1. Introduction

The purpose of this one-on-one mentoring program was to help me grow professionally, deepen my technical knowledge, and prepare for my transition to university. Career preparation was a recurring focus with subjects like how to find a job or internship, how to present myself, and how to think about opportunities strategically.

Going in, I expected a more rigid, planned set of meetings. Instead, our sessions blossomed into organic discussions driven by whatever topics came up naturally. That turned out to be more valuable. One of my biggest takeaways, which I only fully see now in reflection, is just how many resources and opportunities exist online. Certifications like the Google Cybersecurity Professional Certificate, free learning platforms like W3Schools, and recorded conference talks from people working in the industry I want to break into. It pushed me to keep digging, and it reinforced the curiosity that both my mentor and I share when it comes to technology.

## 2. Technical Knowledge Gained

The technical knowledge I gained came largely from the resources Mr. Tonning pointed me toward. The Google Cybersecurity Professional Certificate introduced me to SIEM tools, Python for security, and Linux fundamentals in a structured way that complemented what I had already picked up through CodePath 101, including tools like Metasploit, John the Ripper, and steghide.

What I want to highlight most, though, is the W3Schools Bash exercises. I had some command-line experience and felt reasonably comfortable, but completing those exercises in the first week gave me a stronger foundation and more confidence. Bash scripting is absolutely essential in cybersecurity, and having that base to build on early made everything else click faster, from navigating my home lab to understanding what the Google cert was teaching about Linux.

## 3. Problem Solving and Critical Thinking

The biggest problem-solving challenge I faced during this mentorship wasn't technical. It was practical. Coming in, I was struggling to fit everything into my schedule like coursework, certifications, side projects, and other obligations. I asked Mr. Tonning about the best way to manage it all, and one piece of advice stuck with me.

He reframed the whole idea of time management. It's not about managing time. It's about managing yourself. That shift sounds small, but it changes where you put the emphasis. Time is abstract and outside your control. Yourself is not. Techniques like the Pomodoro method and time-boxing gave me concrete tools, but the reframe is what made them work. It's a struggle I'm still working on, but I'm measurably better at it now than I was ten weeks ago.

## 4. Mentorship and Collaboration

Mr. Tonning's mentoring style worked because it was practical, not theoretical. Instead of just telling me what to do, he showed me. During one meeting, he pulled up a job posting on Handshake for a Junior AI Developer paying $15 to $20 an hour, pasted it into ChatGPT, and walked me through how to evaluate whether it was worth pursuing. That single demo changed how I approach job listings. I now slow down, analyze the requirements against my skills, and make rational decisions instead of reacting emotionally to the title or the pay.

He also brought his own experience into every conversation. Stories about his nephew working at DHS after graduating from WGU, his former student Chris McKenzie building drones in San Francisco, and his own path through certifications and degrees made the career advice feel grounded, not generic. I could see real examples of where the work leads.

The most valuable thing Mr. Tonning did was treat me like a peer. He was honest about what the industry expects, direct about where I needed to improve, and genuinely curious about what I was building on my own, like my NixOS home server. That mutual respect made it easy to be open about what I didn't know.

## 5. Professional Growth

Professional growth is where I improved the most. I started this mentorship with a disabled LinkedIn account, a design-focused resume, and an empty GitHub. I was looking at jobs but not applying to any of them.

Over six weeks, I built a LinkedIn profile, created a GitHub portfolio to document my certifications and technical work, and started actively using Handshake to explore opportunities. More importantly, I learned how to use AI tools to write documentation, analyze job postings, and refine my resume. These are skills that will compound over time.

Beyond the tangible outputs, this experience changed how I relate to professors and faculty. It's easy to put them on a pedestal and keep your distance. Going through this mentorship made me comfortable building deeper connections with other professors and staff, which has already opened doors I didn't expect.

I want to end this section with something I'm proud of. This summer I'm returning to CodePath as a Tech Fellow, not for cybersecurity, but to lead their AI course and help forge the next generation of AI-native software engineers.

## 6. Most Significant Lesson Learned

The most significant lesson I learned during this mentoring experience was to stay curious. I was not told those words directly, but looking back, it was the thread running through everything.

During our second meeting, I was telling Mr. Tonning about all the things I enjoy, my home server, the different Linux distros I run, the projects I want to build, and he told me, "You've got the bug." That stuck with me. In every conversation we had, in meetings and in class, what I admired most about Mr. Tonning was his natural curiosity and his relentless pursuit of learning more.

I have checked into the hotel and I will never check out. Staying curious, continue building, and having fun while doing it.
